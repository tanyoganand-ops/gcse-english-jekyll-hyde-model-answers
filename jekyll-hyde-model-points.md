## Video 1 - Character foils and the people around Jekyll
### J352/01 June 2018 Q13 (extract-based, 40 marks)

- In the extract, Stevenson first fixes Lanyon as orthodoxy made flesh: a hearty, healthy, dapper, red-faced gentleman whose boisterous openness is the opposite of Jekyll's guarded secrecy.
  - "a hearty, healthy, dapper, red-faced gentleman, with a shock of hair prematurely white" - Chapter 2 - Narrator
- Lanyon's science is empirical and conservative: he dismisses Jekyll's research as unscientific balderdash that would have estranged Damon and Pythias, framing a friendship broken on the limits of materialist thought.
  - "Such unscientific balderdash... would have estranged Damon and Pythias." - Chapter 2 - Lanyon
- Jekyll returns the contempt: to him Lanyon is a hide-bound pedant, so the contrast runs both ways - open traditionalism against concealed transgression.
  - "that hide-bound pedant, Lanyon, at what he called my scientific heresies" - Chapter 3 - Jekyll
- The contrast turns fatal: Lanyon's narrow and material views cannot survive empirical proof of the supernatural, so the transformation he witnesses unmakes him while Jekyll accommodates it.
  - "the most narrow and material views, you who have denied the virtue of transcendental medicine" - Chapter 9 - Hyde
- Lanyon's death is therefore the measure of Jekyll's sin: what Jekyll's elastic conscience survives, Lanyon's sickened soul cannot - the honest man dies of what the divided man lives with.
  - "I saw what I saw, I heard what I heard, and my soul sickened at it" - Chapter 9 - Lanyon

### J352/01 June 2019 Q14 (discursive, 40 marks)

- Carew's murder is the plot's decisive engine: the ape-like fury of the killing converts private scandal into public manhunt and forces Hyde underground.
  - "with ape-like fury, he was trampling his victim under foot and hailing down a storm of blows" - Chapter 4 - Narrator
- Stevenson chooses Sir Danvers deliberately: an aged, blameless gentleman makes the crime socially intolerable - the bones audibly shattered raise the stakes from curiosity to justice.
  - "the bones were audibly shattered and the body jumped upon the roadway" - Chapter 4 - Narrator
- Poole moves the story differently, guarding the truth of small facts: he is positive nothing had come except by post, and in that quiet certainty exposes Jekyll's lie about the letter.
  - "positive nothing had come except by post" - Chapter 5 - Poole, reported
- His loyalty then detonates the ending - Down with the door, Poole! - turning suspicion into discovery at the point of an axe.
  - "‘Down with the door, Poole!’... Poole swung the axe over his shoulder" - Chapter 8 - Narrator
- At the climax Poole stands beside Utterson outside the cabinet, hearing only a dismal screech: Stevenson keeps him a witness at the threshold of a truth only Jekyll can narrate - which is precisely how a minor character moves this story along.
  - "A dismal screech, as of mere animal terror, rang from the cabinet." - Chapter 8 - Narrator

### J352/01 June 2025 Q14 (discursive, 40 marks)

- Lanyon is Jekyll's victim first intellectually: Jekyll mocks his friend's orthodoxy as the complaints of a hide-bound pedant, baiting him long before Hyde finishes him.
  - "that hide-bound pedant, Lanyon, at what he called my scientific heresies" - Chapter 3 - Jekyll
- Jekyll, wearing Hyde, weaponises Lanyon's own curiosity: the temptation of a discovery so singular and profound is the bait that draws the honest doctor to his ruin.
  - "the temptation of a discovery so singular and profound at last overcame the suggestions of alarm" - Chapter 10 - Jekyll
- Hyde victimises Lanyon with open cruelty, taunting his narrow and material views in his own drawing room and making him the audience of the impossible.
  - "the most narrow and material views, you who have denied the virtue of transcendental medicine" - Chapter 9 - Hyde
- The victimhood is fatal: the revelation annihilates Lanyon's materialist faith - My life is shaken to its roots; sleep has left me - and he dies of knowledge.
  - "My life is shaken to its roots; sleep has left me" - Chapter 9 - Lanyon
- Yet Stevenson complicates the verdict: Lanyon accepted the wager without knowing its terms, so he is a victim of manipulated consent - ruined by professional curiosity as much as by two men.
  - "I feel that my days are numbered, and that I must die; and yet I shall die incredulous." - Chapter 9 - Lanyon

## Video 2 - Secrecy, pretence and reputation
### J352/12 November 2021 Q7 (extract-based, 40 marks)

- In the extract, Jekyll performs the genial host - pleasant dinners for intelligent, reputable men stage a public self designed to conceal the private one.
  - "one of his pleasant dinners to some five or six old cronies, all intelligent reputable men" - Chapter 3 - Narrator (extract)
- Utterson pretends by omission: he hides what he suspects of Hyde to protect his friend, so pretence becomes loyalty's costume - If he be Mr. Hyde, I shall be Mr. Seek.
  - "If he be Mr. Hyde... I shall be Mr. Seek." - Chapter 2 - Utterson
- Jekyll's experiment institutionalises pretence: Hyde is the impenetrable mantle under which the doctor's desires walk the city unseen.
  - "But for me, in my impenetrable mantle, the safety was complete." - Chapter 10 - Jekyll
- Stevenson roots the pretence in Victorian respectability itself: to wear a more than commonly grave countenance before the public is the age's social contract, not Jekyll's private vice.
  - "wear a more than commonly grave countenance before the public" - Chapter 10 - Jekyll
- Ultimately the pretence fails: a profound duplicity of life cannot be sustained, and the novel's horror is the mask that becomes the face.
  - "I stood already committed to a profound duplicity of life." - Chapter 10 - Jekyll

### J352/01 June 2024 Q13 (extract-based, 40 marks)

- In the extract, Jekyll lies fluently and needlessly - I burned it; the note was handed in - a small deception Poole later exposes, showing secrecy corroding even his oldest friendship.
  - "‘I burned it... The note was handed in.’" - Chapter 5 - Jekyll (extract)
- The letter is deception in material form: Hyde's odd, upright hand signing for Jekyll literalises the double self's forged identities.
  - "The letter was written in an odd, upright hand, and signed ‘Edward Hyde’" - Chapter 5 - Narrator (extract)
- Utterson colludes in the deception: the lawyer liked this letter well enough - secrecy survives because respectability prefers a comfortable lie.
  - "The lawyer liked this letter well enough" - Chapter 5 - Narrator (extract)
- Jekyll's written words make secrecy a test of love: respect my silence converts friendship into an accomplice of concealment.
  - "you can do but one thing, Utterson, to lighten this destiny, and that is to respect my silence" - Chapter 6 - Jekyll's letter
- The novel's very structure is deceptive: Stevenson withholds the truth until the final confession, so the reader shares Utterson's deceived position - a question started like a stone that runs downhill.
  - "You start a question, and it's like starting a stone." - Chapter 1 - Enfield

### J352/01 June 2023 Q13 (extract-based, 40 marks)

- In the extract, reputation governs speech itself: the bargain never to refer to this again values silence above justice.
  - "Let us make a bargain never to refer to this again." - Chapter 1 - Utterson/Enfield (extract)
- Hyde purchases silence through a respectable name: the cheque signed by the very pink of the proprieties shows reputation functioning as currency.
  - "the person that drew the cheque is the very pink of the proprieties" - Chapter 1 - Enfield
- Enfield's threat - to make a name stink from one end of London to the other - exposes scandal as Victorian society's true instrument of discipline.
  - "make his name stink from one end of London to the other" - Chapter 1 - Enfield
- Jekyll's confession admits reputation fathered Hyde: men have before hired bravos to transact their crimes while their own person and reputation sat under shelter.
  - "Men have before hired bravos to transact their crimes, while their own person and reputation sat under shelter." - Chapter 10 - Jekyll
- Stevenson judges the cost: reputation guarded at the price of truth breeds the profound duplicity that destroys Jekyll - the novel's indictment of the grave countenance of the age.
  - "wear a more than commonly grave countenance before the public" - Chapter 10 - Jekyll

## Video 3 - Utterson, silence and control
### J352/01 June 2019 Q13 (extract-based, 40 marks)

- In the extract, Jekyll's refusal to descend - no, no, no; it is quite impossible; I dare not - is speech stifled by terror: silence as the symptom of his secret.
  - "‘no, no, no; it is quite impossible; I dare not’" - Chapter 7 - Jekyll (extract)
- Utterson practises a chosen speechlessness: his code - I let my brother go to the devil in his own way - makes non-interference a form of loyalty.
  - "I incline to Cain's heresy... I let my brother go to the devil in his own way." - Chapter 1 - Utterson
- Enfield names the danger of speech itself: you start a question, and it's like starting a stone - one inquiry sets the whole avalanche of discovery moving.
  - "You start a question, and it's like starting a stone." - Chapter 1 - Enfield
- Jekyll's letter converts silence into command: respect my silence elevates concealment to the condition of friendship.
  - "you can do but one thing, Utterson, to lighten this destiny, and that is to respect my silence" - Chapter 6 - Jekyll's letter
- Stevenson makes silence Gothic: the door with neither bell nor knocker, blistered and distained, withholds speech as its inhabitants do.
  - "The door, which was equipped with neither bell nor knocker, was blistered and distained." - Chapter 1 - Narrator

### J352/01 June 2020 Q13 (extract-based, 40 marks)

- In the extract, Stevenson builds Utterson from contradictions: austere with himself yet tolerant of others, a man who drinks gin to mortify a taste for vintages.
  - "He was austere with himself; drank gin when he was alone, to mortify a taste for vintages" - Chapter 1 - Narrator (extract)
- His morality is deliberately passive: I incline to Cain's heresy - a lawyer who refuses judgment, which makes him the last good influence in the lives of down-going men.
  - "the last good influence in the lives of downgoing men" - Chapter 1 - Narrator
- Utterson's friendships are accidents of endurance, not choice: his affections, like ivy, were the growth of time - Stevenson makes loyalty a habit rather than a passion.
  - "his affections, like ivy, were the growth of time" - Chapter 1 - Narrator
- Elsewhere the passive man becomes the detective: If he be Mr. Hyde, I shall be Mr. Seek - curiosity finally overrides his code of non-interference.
  - "If he be Mr. Hyde... I shall be Mr. Seek." - Chapter 2 - Utterson
- Stevenson uses him as the novel's measuring instrument: an unimpeachably ordinary conscience, so that the horror of Jekyll's secret is gauged by what it costs this dull, faithful friend.
  - "lean, long, dusty, dreary and yet somehow lovable" - Chapter 1 - Narrator

### J352/01 June 2020 Q14 (discursive, 40 marks)

- Jekyll's experiment is a bid for absolute self-command: to house each impulse in separate identities and be master of both.
  - "If each... could but be housed in separate identities, life would be relieved of all that was unbearable" - Chapter 10 - Jekyll
- The potion sells an illusion of control - the moment I choose, I can be rid of Mr. Hyde - a boast the plot systematically dismantles.
  - "the moment I choose, I can be rid of Mr. Hyde" - Chapter 3 - Jekyll
- Control becomes addiction: my new power tempted me until I fell in slavery recasts mastery as enslavement.
  - "my new power tempted me until I fell in slavery" - Chapter 10 - Jekyll
- The counter-desire is the novel's real subject: Hyde embodies the wish to escape control - to strip off these lendings and spring headlong into the sea of liberty.
  - "strip off these lendings and spring headlong into the sea of liberty" - Chapter 10 - Jekyll
- Stevenson ends control in the forfeiture of will: the power of voluntary change is lost, and Jekyll wakes to find himself the prisoner of the self he made.
  - "the power of voluntary change be forfeited, and the character of Edward Hyde become irrevocably mine" - Chapter 10 - Jekyll

## Video 4 - Duality and sympathy
### J352/01 June 2023 Q14 (discursive, 40 marks)

- Physically the statement holds: Jekyll shows every mark of capacity and kindness while Hyde gives a strong feeling of deformity - bodies written as moral opposites.
  - "a large, well-made, smooth-faced man of fifty... every mark of capacity and kindness" - Chapter 3 - Narrator
- Morally they diverge absolutely: Hyde, alone in the ranks of mankind, was pure evil, while Jekyll remains commingled - divided, not damned.
  - "Edward Hyde, alone in the ranks of mankind, was pure evil." - Chapter 10 - Jekyll
- Yet they share one will and one memory: Jekyll greets his other self with a leap of welcome, not horror - complicity, not opposition.
  - "when I looked upon that ugly idol in the glass, I was conscious of no repugnance, rather of a leap of welcome" - Chapter 10 - Jekyll
- The transformation scene collapses the difference entirely: before Lanyon's eyes, there stood Henry Jekyll where Hyde had been - one body, two names.
  - "there stood Henry Jekyll!" - Chapter 9 - Lanyon's account
- Stevenson's deepest point denies the statement: the two are polar twins of one consciousness - difference within a single man, not between two men.
  - "these polar twins should be continuously struggling" - Chapter 10 - Jekyll

### J352/12 June 2022 Q8 (discursive, 40 marks)

- Jekyll courts sympathy as a prisoner of his own nature: If I am the chief of sinners, I am the chief of sufferers also.
  - "If I am the chief of sinners, I am the chief of sufferers also." - Chapter 6 - Jekyll's letter
- His torment is rendered physically - a grinding in the bones, deadly nausea, and a horror of the spirit - suffering that legitimately earns pity.
  - "a grinding in the bones, deadly nausea, and a horror of the spirit" - Chapter 10 - Jekyll
- Yet sympathy founders on responsibility: he concealed his pleasures knowingly and fell in slavery by choice, not by chance.
  - "Hence it came about that I concealed my pleasures" - Chapter 10 - Jekyll
- The Carew murder tests the limit: Jekyll hates and fears the brute within, yet goes on - and pity struggles to survive his complicity.
  - "I still hated and feared the thought of the brute that slept within me" - Chapter 10 - Jekyll
- Stevenson balances the verdict: I was slowly losing hold of my original and better self reads at once as confession and excuse - Jekyll is both victim of a repressive age and author of his ruin.
  - "I was slowly losing hold of my original and better self, and becoming slowly incorporated with my second and worse" - Chapter 10 - Jekyll

### J352/12 November 2021 Q8 (discursive, 40 marks)

- The pun is the novel's architecture: Hyde is both hider and hidden - Jekyll wears him as an impenetrable mantle, and inside that disguise the doctor's guilt stays out of sight.
  - "But for me, in my impenetrable mantle, the safety was complete." - Chapter 10 - Jekyll
- He hides Jekyll's guilt: the respectable name signs the cheques for Hyde's crimes while Jekyll's person and reputation sat under shelter.
  - "Men have before hired bravos to transact their crimes, while their own person and reputation sat under shelter." - Chapter 10 - Jekyll
- Hyde hides from every witness: he gives a strong feeling of deformity, although no one can specify the point - a face that refuses description is a face that refuses capture.
  - "he gives a strong feeling of deformity, although I couldn't specify the point" - Chapter 1 - Enfield
- The hidden truth is identity itself: the revelation that there stood Henry Jekyll shows the hider and the hidden are one man.
  - "there stood Henry Jekyll!" - Chapter 9 - Lanyon's account
- Beyond agreement lies irony: in the end Hyde refuses to hide - it was always as Hyde that I awakened - so the name fails precisely where it promised safety.
  - "if I slept, or even dozed for a moment in my chair, it was always as Hyde that I awakened" - Chapter 10 - Jekyll

## Video 5 - Shock and terror
### J352/12 June 2022 Q7 (extract-based, 40 marks)

- In the extract, the transformation is staged as spectacle: his face became suddenly black, and the features seemed to melt and alter - shock filtered through Lanyon's horrified gaze.
  - "his face became suddenly black, and the features seemed to melt and alter" - Chapter 9 - Lanyon's account (extract)
- The shock is intellectual as much as visual: for Lanyon, the man of narrow and material views, the incident annihilates a whole worldview - his life shaken to its roots.
  - "My life is shaken to its roots; sleep has left me" - Chapter 9 - Lanyon
- The Carew murder is the novel's other great shock: ape-like fury and audibly shattered bones convert rumoured threat into witnessed atrocity.
  - "with ape-like fury, he was trampling his victim under foot" - Chapter 4 - Narrator
- Shock also works quietly: a man who tramples calmly over a child's body horrifies through composure, not spectacle - It sounds nothing to hear, but it was hellish to see.
  - "It sounds nothing to hear, but it was hellish to see." - Chapter 1 - Enfield
- Finally, Stevenson makes shock moral as well as physical: Lanyon sickens not at violence but at knowledge - in this reading, the revelation itself is the wound.
  - "I saw what I saw, I heard what I heard, and my soul sickened at it" - Chapter 9 - Lanyon

### J352/01 June 2025 Q13 (extract-based, 40 marks)

- In the extract, terror precedes understanding: the dismal screech, as of mere animal terror, frightens before anything is seen - fear of the unknown behind the door.
  - "A dismal screech, as of mere animal terror, rang from the cabinet." - Chapter 8 - Narrator (extract)
- Stevenson inverts heroism: the besiegers are appalled by their own riot - terror belongs as much to those breaking in as to the thing within.
  - "The besiegers, appalled by their own riot and the stillness that had succeeded, stood back a little and peered in." - Chapter 8 - Narrator (extract)
- Elsewhere terror is atmospheric: the nightmare street and the great chocolate-coloured pall make London itself an object of dread.
  - "A great chocolate-coloured pall lowered over heaven" - Chapter 4 - Narrator
- The window scene distils terror into a single glimpse: such abject terror and despair, as froze the very blood of the two gentlemen below.
  - "such abject terror and despair, as froze the very blood of the two gentlemen below" - Chapter 7 - Narrator
- Jekyll's confession gives terror its final form - not violence but the loss of self: solely occupied by one thought: the horror of my other self.
  - "solely occupied by one thought: the horror of my other self" - Chapter 10 - Jekyll

## Video 6 - Justice and turning points
### J352/01 June 2018 Q14 (discursive, 40 marks)

- The murder converts private brutality into public crime: before it, Hyde's violence is a half-witnessed outrage hushed up with money; after it, he is a hunted murderer - the hinge on which the plot turns.
  - "Mr. Hyde broke out of all bounds and clubbed him to the earth." - Chapter 4 - Narrator
- Its savagery marks the point of no return: with ape-like fury proves Hyde's violence escalating beyond any control Jekyll can claim.
  - "with ape-like fury, he was trampling his victim under foot and hailing down a storm of blows" - Chapter 4 - Narrator
- The murder provokes Jekyll's last reform: it triggers the desperate, failing effort to keep Hyde caged - I have brought on myself a punishment and a danger that I cannot name.
  - "I have brought on myself a punishment and a danger that I cannot name." - Chapter 6 - Jekyll's letter
- Yet the revelation rivals it as a turning point for the reader: only Lanyon's narrative, set down after the murder, confirms what Hyde is - the murder turns the plot; the letter turns our knowledge.
  - "there stood Henry Jekyll!" - Chapter 9 - Lanyon's account
- Stevenson frames the murder as moral fulcrum rather than climax: it turns the story from mystery into tragedy - from what Hyde is to what Jekyll must pay.
  - "Instantly the spirit of hell awoke in me and raged." - Chapter 10 - Jekyll

### J352/01 June 2024 Q14 (discursive, 40 marks)

- On one reading, justice is served: the man who hired sin under shelter dies by the logic of his own potion - his punishment self-inflicted.
  - "Men have before hired bravos to transact their crimes, while their own person and reputation sat under shelter." - Chapter 10 - Jekyll
- Jekyll himself accepts the verdict: I have brought on myself a punishment and a danger that I cannot name - confession as self-sentencing.
  - "I have brought on myself a punishment and a danger that I cannot name." - Chapter 6 - Jekyll's letter
- Yet the end punishes a universal condition: all human beings are commingled out of good and evil - Jekyll suffers for what every reader shares.
  - "all human beings, as we meet them, are commingled out of good and evil" - Chapter 10 - Jekyll
- The timing denies justice its clarity: it may be Hyde, not Jekyll, who dies in the cabinet - the character of Edward Hyde become irrevocably mine leaves the verdict deliberately unresolved.
  - "the character of Edward Hyde become irrevocably mine" - Chapter 10 - Jekyll
- The novel's Christian frame complicates desert altogether: in the law of God, there is no statute of limitations - judgment lies beyond any ending Stevenson shows us.
  - "in the law of God, there is no statute of limitations" - Chapter 2 - Utterson's thoughts
